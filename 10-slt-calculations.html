Module 3 • Planning and Designing Your ODL Course

SLT Calculations & Worked Examples

Practising the calculations that determine whether your course qualifies as ODL

On the previous page, you learned what Student Learning Time is, how it maps to credits, and which activities count towards the ODL delivery requirement. This page is about putting that knowledge into practice. You will work through the process of estimating SLT for individual activities, assembling those into a complete course profile, and then calculating the ODL percentage. Along the way, you will see two fully worked examples — one that qualifies and one that does not — so you can compare what separates a compliant design from one that falls short.

Duration Does Not Equal SLT

One of the most common mistakes in ODL planning is treating the duration of a learning resource as the SLT allocation. A 1-hour recorded lecture does not automatically equal 1 hour of Student Learning Time. Students do not simply press play and listen — they pause, rewind, take notes, re-watch difficult sections, consult supplementary readings, and complete associated activities.

SLT must reflect the total time reasonably required for a student to achieve the intended learning outcomes through meaningful engagement with the material. This includes viewing or reading, processing, practising, discussing, reflecting, and preparing assessments.

Avoid the duration trap. Assigning SLT solely based on the length of videos or online lectures will understate the actual student workload and may lead to a course that does not meet its credit-value obligations. Always consider the complete learning experience, not just the media runtime.

An SLT Equivalency Reference

The table below provides a general guide for estimating SLT from common ODL activities. These are indicative ranges — the actual allocation depends on the complexity of the material, the level of the course, and the specific learning activities designed around each resource.

Learning Activity Resource / Input Estimated SLT What the SLT Includes
Recorded lecture (video) 1 hour of video 2 – 3 hours Viewing, pausing, note-taking, re-watching key sections, completing embedded questions
Instructional video (short) 15 – 20 min video 45 – 60 min Viewing, processing, applying the concept to a short exercise
Academic reading 10 pages of text 2 – 3 hours Reading, annotating, looking up references, summarising key arguments
Discussion forum 1 structured discussion 1 – 2 hours Reading peers' posts, composing a response, providing feedback, follow-up replies
Online quiz (formative) 10 – 15 questions 45 – 60 min Reviewing relevant material, attempting the quiz, reviewing feedback
Assignment preparation One written assignment 8 – 15 hours Research, reading, drafting, revising, formatting, submission
Synchronous online session 1 hour live session 1 – 1.5 hours Live participation plus pre-reading or post-session consolidation
E-portfolio entry One reflective entry 2 – 4 hours Reflection, evidence gathering, writing, uploading artefacts
These are guides, not rules. The exact SLT for each activity depends on your course context, the complexity of the material, and what you ask students to do with it. The important thing is that your allocations are reasonable, defensible, and clearly documented in your SIM and Table 4.

A Three-Step Calculation Process

Calculating SLT and the ODL percentage for your course involves three steps. Every ODL course design follows this same sequence:

1

Allocate SLT

Estimate the total hours for each activity using the equivalency guide. Sum to match the course credit value.

2

Classify as ODL or Not

Mark each activity as ODL delivery (sync, async, structured independent, online assessment) or non-ODL (physical F2F, unstructured independent).

3

Calculate the Percentage

Sum ODL hours ÷ total SLT × 100. Check that the result is at least 80% and that physical F2F does not exceed 20%.

This course fails on two counts: the ODL percentage (54.2%) is well below 80%, and the physical face-to-face component (29.2%) exceeds the 20% cap. The large block of on-campus workshops and the unstructured independent learning are the main contributors. Let's look at what careful SLT estimation looks like in practice — specifically, how an experienced lecturer avoids the duration trap.

Good Practice: Dr. Mei Lin's Estimation Process

Dr. Mei Lin is allocating SLT for a recorded video lecture on regression analysis in her MSc Data Analytics course. The video is 45 minutes long. Rather than assigning 45 minutes of SLT, she considers what students will actually do with the video.

Her SLT breakdown for one 45-minute video:

  • First viewing (with pausing and note-taking): 75 minutes
  • Re-watching key sections on standard error and confidence intervals: 15 minutes
  • Completing the embedded practice dataset and checking answers: 30 minutes
  • Posting a question or reflection to the discussion forum: 15 minutes
  • Total SLT for this single video: 2 hours 15 minutes

She documents this breakdown in her SIM so that both the instructional designer and the MQA assessor can see how the allocation was derived.

Why this works: Dr. Mei Lin considers the complete learning experience — not just the video runtime. Her SLT is approximately 3 times the video duration, which is consistent with the equivalency guide. She also documents her reasoning, making the allocation transparent and defensible during accreditation. Participants in her course will not be surprised by the workload because the expectations are clearly stated upfront.

Now let's see what happens when this careful estimation process is skipped.

Case: Prof. Hassan's Shortcut

Prof. Hassan is designing a 3-credit ODL course (120 SLT hours). He has 20 hours of recorded video lectures, so he allocates exactly 20 hours of SLT for "asynchronous online learning." He also has 15 hours of live online tutorials (SLT = 15 hours), 40 hours of independent reading (SLT = 40 hours, all counted as ODL), 25 hours of physical lab sessions (SLT = 25 hours), and 20 hours of assessment (SLT = 20 hours). His ODL calculation: 20 + 15 + 40 = 75 hours ÷ 120 = 62.5%. He is confused — he thought having plenty of recorded content would make the course ODL.

What went wrong? Prof. Hassan made three errors. First, he assigned SLT equal to video duration (20 hours of video = 20 hours SLT), ignoring the additional time students spend pausing, note-taking, and completing activities. Using the equivalency guide, 20 hours of recorded lectures should yield approximately 40–60 hours of SLT. Second, he counted all 40 hours of independent reading as ODL without verifying that they are integrated into the LMS. Third, his physical F2F component (25 hours = 20.8%) slightly exceeds the 20% cap. If he corrected the video SLT to 40 hours (using a 2x multiplier), properly classified his independent learning (say, 25 of 40 hours are LMS-integrated), and reduced F2F to 24 hours, his revised ODL calculation would be: 15 (sync) + 40 (async) + 25 (structured independent) + 20 (assessment) = 100 ÷ 120 = 83.3%. The course would qualify.

Quiz: Spot the SLT Error

Dr. Lim has recorded 10 one-hour video lectures for her ODL course. In her Table 4, she allocates exactly 10 hours of SLT for "asynchronous online learning" based on these videos. She states: "10 hours of video content = 10 hours of asynchronous SLT." What is the fundamental problem with this allocation?

  • C. Video lectures cannot count as ODL delivery unless they are accompanied by physical face-to-face sessions
  • D. The 10 hours should be split evenly between asynchronous and independent learning categories

Summary

SLT allocation is about estimating the total time a student reasonably needs to engage with each learning activity — not about measuring the duration of a video or the length of a reading. The equivalency guide provides starting points, but your own course context determines the final numbers. Once SLT is allocated and classified, the ODL percentage follows from a straightforward calculation: ODL hours divided by total SLT.
