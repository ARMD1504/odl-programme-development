Module 3 • Planning and Designing Your ODL Course

Table 4 — Your Course Blueprint

The single document that ties together your learning outcomes, activities, assessments, and SLT

On the previous two pages, you learned how to allocate and calculate Student Learning Time. Those calculations are essential — but they do not exist in isolation. They live inside a document called Table 4, which is the primary course planning tool required by MQA for every ODL course. Table 4 is where your course design comes together: learning outcomes, teaching and learning activities, assessment methods, and SLT allocation all documented in a single, structured format. Understanding how to complete Table 4 correctly is the difference between a course that passes accreditation review and one that gets sent back for revision.

What Is Table 4?

Table 4 is the Course Information template prescribed by the Malaysian Qualifications Agency (MQA) under the Code of Practice for Programme Accreditation: Open and Distance Learning (COPPA:ODL). It is the official course-level document that describes your course structure, learning outcomes, teaching and learning activities, assessment methods, and Student Learning Time distribution.

For ODL courses, Table 4 serves multiple purposes simultaneously. It is a planning tool that helps you design your course coherently. It is an accreditation document that MQA assessors review during programme approval and audit exercises. And it is a communication tool that tells students, colleagues, and quality assurance reviewers exactly what your course involves and how it is delivered.

Table 4 is not optional. Academic staff are required to complete Table 4 in accordance with MQA's prescribed format. For ODL courses, the SLT distribution documented in Table 4 provides the basis for determining whether the course satisfies the 80% ODL delivery requirement. Incomplete or inconsistent Table 4 documentation is one of the most common reasons programmes are flagged during accreditation.

The Alignment Chain

Table 4 is built around a principle called constructive alignment: every component of your course must connect logically to every other component. Your learning outcomes determine what activities students do. Those activities determine how you assess. And all of the above determine how SLT is distributed. If any link in this chain is broken, the course design is incoherent — and Table 4 will reveal the gap.

When you fill in Table 4, you are essentially documenting this chain. Each CLO should map to specific activities, which in turn map to specific assessments, which collectively account for the total SLT. MQA assessors look for this alignment — and they will question any CLO that has no corresponding activity, any activity that is not assessed, or any SLT hours that do not connect to a learning outcome.

Key Sections of Table 4 for ODL Courses

While Table 4 contains several sections covering different aspects of course information, the following sections are particularly important for ODL course design:

Section

Course Information

Course title, code, credit value, prerequisite, synopsis, and the programme it belongs to. The credit value determines your total SLT (credits × 40 hours).

Section

Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs)

The specific outcomes students must achieve, mapped to programme learning outcomes and MQF domains. Each CLO drives the activities and assessments that follow.

Section

Teaching & Learning Activities

The methods used to deliver the course: synchronous online, asynchronous online, independent learning, and physical face-to-face. Each activity should link to one or more CLOs.

Section

Assessment Methods

How each CLO is assessed: online quizzes, assignments, e-portfolios, discussions, presentations, or examinations. Must align with both the CLOs and the activities.

Section 10

Student Learning Time (SLT) Distribution

The breakdown of total SLT across all activity types. For ODL courses, this section must show that at least 80% of SLT is delivered through ODL approaches. This is the section that determines your course's ODL classification.

Section

Learning Resources

Core and supplementary readings, digital resources, SIM references, and any specialised materials. Should be consistent with the activities described above.

Section 10 is the critical section for ODL compliance. This is where you document how many hours are allocated to each delivery method and where the 80% ODL threshold is verified. All the SLT calculation skills you practised on the previous pages are applied in this section.

From Blueprint to Delivery

Table 4 does not exist in isolation. It is the first step in a three-stage pipeline that moves from planning to implementation to delivery. Once your Table 4 is approved, it becomes the blueprint for developing your Self-Instructional Materials (SIMs). Those SIMs are then implemented within the Learning Management System (LMS), which is where students actually experience the course.

This pipeline matters because every stage must be consistent with the one before it. Your SIM should accurately reflect what Table 4 specifies. Your LMS should deliver what the SIM contains. If Table 4 allocates 28 hours of asynchronous learning, but the SIM only provides 15 hours of asynchronous content, the discrepancy will be flagged during quality review. Similarly, if the LMS implementation diverges from the approved SIM, the course is no longer delivering what was planned.

Consistency is non-negotiable. MQA assessors will compare your Table 4, your SIM, and your LMS implementation. Any mismatch between what was planned (Table 4), what was developed (SIM), and what students actually experience (LMS) is a compliance risk. Keep all three documents aligned throughout the development process.

Let's look at how an experienced lecturer uses Table 4 effectively during the course design process.

Good Practice: Dr. Priya's Table 4 Process

Dr. Priya is developing an ODL course in Educational Research Methods (3 credits, 120 SLT hours). Rather than filling in Table 4 as an afterthought, she uses it as her primary design tool from the very beginning.

Her process:

  • She starts with the CLOs — writing them first, before deciding on any activities or assessments
  • For each CLO, she identifies the teaching and learning activities that will help students achieve it, noting whether each activity is synchronous, asynchronous, independent, or physical F2F
  • For each CLO, she selects an assessment method that directly measures whether students have achieved that outcome
  • She then allocates SLT hours to each activity using the equivalency guide, ensuring the total matches her 3-credit value (120 hours)
  • Finally, she calculates the ODL percentage from the SLT breakdown in Section 10 — and confirms it meets the 80% threshold before proceeding to SIM development
Why this works: Dr. Priya uses Table 4 as a design tool, not just a compliance document. By starting with CLOs and working forward through activities, assessments, and SLT, she ensures constructive alignment from the outset. Her ODL percentage is verified before she begins developing SIMs, which means she does not have to restructure her course later. This forward-planning approach prevents the common problem of building a course first and then trying to retrofit it into Table 4 — which almost always produces alignment gaps.

Now let's see what happens when Table 4 is treated as a compliance exercise rather than a design tool.

Case: Dr. Wong's Misaligned Table 4

Dr. Wong has been asked to convert his face-to-face course into an ODL course. He develops his SIM and LMS content first, building what he thinks is a good online course. Only after the LMS is set up does he sit down to fill in Table 4. He quickly discovers several problems: his CLOs mention "critical evaluation" but his activities are mostly recorded lectures with no evaluation exercises; his SLT allocation adds up to 140 hours for a 3-credit course (he forgot the credit-to-hours constraint); and his assessment section lists a final examination that does not map to any specific CLO. The ODL percentage in Section 10 calculates to 76%, below the 80% threshold. He now faces the prospect of restructuring his entire course to fix the alignment gaps.

What went wrong? Dr. Wong treated Table 4 as an afterthought — a form to fill in after the course was already built. Because he did not use Table 4 to guide his design decisions from the start, the resulting course had misaligned CLOs, overallocated SLT, unmapped assessments, and an ODL percentage that failed to meet the threshold. Had he started with Table 4, he would have caught all of these issues during the planning stage, before investing time in SIM development and LMS implementation.

Summary

Table 4 is the single document that ties together every aspect of your ODL course design: learning outcomes, activities, assessments, and SLT allocation. It should be used as a design tool from the start, not filled in retrospectively. The alignment chain (CLOs → activities → assessments → SLT) ensures that every component connects logically, and the SLT distribution in Section 10 determines whether your course meets the 80% ODL threshold. Once approved, Table 4 becomes the blueprint for developing your SIMs and implementing them on the LMS.
