Module 3 • Planning and Designing Your ODL Course

LMS Implementation & Accreditation Readiness

Moving from your Table 4 blueprint to a live course on the LMS — and preparing for accreditation review

On the previous page, you learned how Table 4 serves as the blueprint for your ODL course. You now have a complete plan: learning outcomes, activities, assessments, and SLT allocation, all documented and aligned. The next step is to bring that plan to life on the Learning Management System — the platform where students will actually experience your course. This page covers what effective LMS implementation looks like, what evidence you need to maintain, and the accreditation requirements that govern new ODL programmes.

The LMS Is Not a File Repository

The LMS serves as the University's primary ODL delivery platform. It is the environment through which students access learning materials, engage in activities, communicate with lecturers and peers, submit assessments, receive feedback, and monitor their learning progress.

A common mistake is to treat the LMS as a digital filing cabinet — uploading lecture slides, PDFs, and recorded videos and expecting students to navigate the content on their own. This approach creates a content dump, not a learning experience. The LMS should instead provide a structured, interactive environment that supports student-centred, self-directed learning and active engagement.

Uploading is not implementing. MQA assessors distinguish between a course that has been uploaded to the LMS and a course that has been implemented on the LMS. Implementation means the learning experience is structured, navigable, interactive, and consistent with what Table 4 specifies. A folder of lecture slides does not constitute implementation.

Implementation Requirements

When building your course on the LMS, the following requirements should guide your design. Each of these connects back to your Table 4 — they are the practical expression of your course blueprint in the online environment:

LMS Implementation Checklist

  • Course content, learning activities, assessments, and SLT expectations are consistent with the approved Table 4
  • Content is organised into clearly labelled weekly or topic-based learning modules
  • Learning outcomes, learning activities, and assessments are clearly aligned within each module
  • Learning materials and resources are easy to locate and navigate
  • Instructions and learning pathways are clear and concise
  • Hyperlinks, multimedia resources, and embedded content are functional and accessible
  • Assessment submission points, rubrics, and feedback mechanisms are set up and tested
  • Communication tools (announcements, discussion forums, messaging) are active and structured
Consistency with Table 4 is the golden rule. If your Table 4 allocates 28 hours of asynchronous online learning, your LMS should contain approximately 28 hours' worth of asynchronous content and activities. If Table 4 specifies an e-portfolio assessment, the LMS should include the instructions, rubric, and submission mechanism. Any mismatch will be flagged during quality review.

Evidence and Records for Quality Assurance

For quality assurance, audit, accreditation, and continuous improvement purposes, you need to maintain evidence that your LMS implementation is active and effective. This is not about creating extra documentation — it is about ensuring that the LMS itself generates and retains records that demonstrate the course is being delivered as planned.

Student Engagement Data

Login frequency, content access patterns, discussion forum participation, and time spent on learning activities

Assessment Records

Submission timestamps, grades, feedback provided, and evidence of academic integrity measures

Communication Logs

Announcements posted, discussion forum threads, lecturer responses, and consultation records

Content Analytics

Module completion rates, resource download statistics, and quiz attempt patterns

These records serve a dual purpose. They provide evidence for MQA assessors that the SIM has been effectively implemented and that students have been provided with an active, supported online learning environment. They also give you data for Continuous Quality Improvement — helping you identify which modules students struggle with, which resources are underused, and where the course design needs refinement.

There is one additional dimension that applies specifically to new programmes seeking accreditation for the first time.

Provisional Accreditation (PA) Requirement

For new ODL programmes seeking Provisional Accreditation, MQA requires evidence that the programme is supported by adequately developed learning materials. At the point of PA application submission, the following conditions must be met:

  • At least one semester's worth of completed SIMs is developed and available for review
  • The SIMs are fully implemented within the LMS — not limited to planning documents or outlines
  • The SIMs demonstrate the intended ODL learning experience, including content, activities, assessments, and learner support
  • The SIMs are aligned with the approved curriculum, CLOs, SLT, and ODL delivery requirements
  • Access can be provided to MQA assessors for evaluation during the PA exercise

In practice, this means MQA will not accredit a new ODL programme based on plans alone. They need to see actual, implemented course materials on the LMS — the real learning experience students will encounter — before granting provisional approval. The one-semester requirement allows MQA to evaluate programme readiness before full implementation begins.

Let's see how these principles play out in practice — and what happens when they are overlooked.

Case: Dr. Hassan's File Dump

Dr. Hassan has completed his Table 4 and is now implementing his ODL course on the LMS. He creates a single folder called "Course Materials" and uploads all 14 weeks of lecture slides as PDFs, along with a reading list and three assignment briefs. He sets up a single discussion forum titled "General Discussion" and considers the LMS setup complete. When the quality assurance team reviews his course, they flag multiple issues: the LMS content does not match the weekly module structure described in Table 4, there are no self-assessment activities, and the discussion forum has no prompts or facilitation plan.

What went wrong? Dr. Hassan treated the LMS as a file repository rather than a structured learning environment. His Table 4 describes weekly modules with specific learning outcomes, activities, and SLT allocations — but none of that structure is reflected on the LMS. The uploaded slides are lecture support materials, not self-instructional resources. There are no embedded quizzes, no guided activities, no reflection prompts, and no structured discussion topics. A student logging into this course would see a folder of files but no learning pathway. Dr. Hassan needs to restructure the LMS into weekly modules that mirror his Table 4, add interactive elements, set up structured assessments with rubrics, and create facilitated discussion forums with clear prompts and participation expectations.

With the implementation requirements and accreditation context clear, let's test your understanding of what effective LMS implementation involves.

Check Your Understanding

A lecturer has completed Table 4 for her new ODL course. Her Table 4 specifies 14 weekly modules, each with clearly stated CLOs, structured asynchronous activities, self-assessment quizzes, and a cumulative e-portfolio assessment. She now implements the course on the LMS. Which of the following approaches best demonstrates effective LMS implementation?

  • A. She uploads all lecture slides into a single "Resources" folder and creates one submission point for the final e-portfolio, allowing students to access everything at their own pace
  • B. She creates 14 weekly modules on the LMS, each containing the stated CLO, guided learning materials, structured activities, a self-assessment quiz, and the relevant e-portfolio milestone — matching her Table 4 structure exactly
  • C. She records 14 video lectures covering all content and uploads them sequentially, with a final examination at the end of the semester
  • D. She builds the modules on a separate website and provides students with a link, keeping the LMS for administrative announcements only

Summary

The LMS is where your course plan becomes a real learning experience for students. Effective implementation means structuring the LMS to mirror your Table 4 — weekly modules with aligned outcomes, activities, assessments, and SLT expectations. The LMS must also generate engagement evidence for quality assurance and accreditation purposes. For new programmes, at least one semester of fully implemented SIMs on the LMS is required before Provisional Accreditation can be granted.
