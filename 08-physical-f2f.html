Module 2 • Delivery Methods

Physical Face-to-Face Sessions

The only delivery method that does not count as ODL — and why it is still sometimes necessary

You have now covered all three online delivery methods: synchronous, asynchronous, and independent learning. Each contributes to ODL delivery in a different way. This page covers the fourth and final method: physical face-to-face sessions. Unlike the online methods, physical F2F does not count as ODL delivery. Yet it remains an important — and sometimes essential — component of many ODL courses. Understanding when and how to include it, and where the limits are, is critical for your course design.

What Are Physical Face-to-Face Sessions?

Physical face-to-face learning refers to teaching and learning activities where academic staff and students are physically present at the same location and interact directly in person. These activities are conducted in traditional learning environments such as classrooms, laboratories, studios, workshops, clinical facilities, simulation centres, or field sites.

While ODL programmes primarily rely on online and distance learning approaches, limited physical face-to-face activities may be included to support the achievement of learning outcomes that cannot be effectively delivered through online methods alone.

The fundamental distinction: Online methods deliver learning through digital technologies — that is what makes them ODL. Physical F2F delivers learning through physical co-location — that is what makes it not ODL. The question is not whether physical sessions are valuable (they often are), but whether they should count towards your ODL delivery percentage (they should not).

When Are Physical F2F Sessions Appropriate?

Physical face-to-face sessions should only be included in an ODL course where there is a clear justification. The guideline recognises four types of justification:

Acceptable Justifications

Pedagogical

The learning outcome requires hands-on experience, physical manipulation, or in-person demonstration that cannot be replicated online.

Practical

Specialised equipment, facilities, or environments are required (labs, studios, clinical settings).

Professional

Professional accreditation or competency standards require in-person assessment or demonstration.

Regulatory

Government, accreditation, or licensing bodies mandate physical attendance for specific components.

If your reason for including physical sessions does not fall into one of these categories, reconsider whether the activity could be redesigned for online delivery.

What Physical F2F Counts As

Physical Face-to-Face Sessions Count As:

Contact Hours Student Learning Time ODL Delivery

Physical F2F sessions are recognised as traditional contact hours because academic staff and students engage directly in the same physical environment. They also form part of the total SLT of the course. However, they are not considered ODL delivery because the learning takes place through physical attendance rather than online or distance learning approaches.

The consequence: Every hour of physical F2F reduces the proportion of ODL delivery in your course. If your physical F2F exceeds 20% of total SLT, your course may fail the 80% ODL threshold and lose its ODL classification.

The 20% Cap

For an ODL course, physical face-to-face activities should generally not exceed 20% of the total Student Learning Time. The majority of learning activities should be delivered through online and distance learning approaches.

Designing with Physical F2F in Mind

You now understand all four delivery methods and how they interact with the ODL classification rules. The practical challenge is balancing them in your course design. Physical F2F is not forbidden — it is simply capped and must be justified. Case: The 25% Problem

Dr. Lim designs a 4-credit ODL course (160 hours SLT) for a nursing programme. She includes 20 hours of synchronous online case discussions, 30 hours of asynchronous learning modules, 30 hours of guided independent study, 40 hours of on-campus clinical simulation labs, and 40 hours of hospital-based practicum. Her total physical F2F is 80 hours out of 160 — that is 50% of the SLT. She argues that nursing requires hands-on clinical practice, so the high F2F component is justified.

What's the issue? Dr. Lim's pedagogical justification is valid — nursing does require hands-on clinical practice. However, at 50% physical F2F, her ODL delivery is only 80 hours out of 160 (50%), which falls far below the 80% threshold. This course cannot be classified as an ODL course. Dr. Lim has two options: (1) accept that this is a conventional course with an ODL component rather than an ODL course, or (2) restructure the programme so that this high-F2F course is balanced by other courses with very high ODL delivery, allowing the programme overall to still meet the 60% threshold.

This case illustrates an important principle: not every course in an ODL programme needs to be an ODL course. The programme-level classification depends on graduating credits, and some courses may legitimately remain conventional if their subject matter requires extensive physical presence.

Check Your Understanding

Dr. Aminah is designing a 3-credit ODL course (120 hours SLT). She plans 28 hours of live online seminars, 22 hours of recorded modules, 30 hours of guided independent reading, 20 hours of online discussion forums, and 20 hours of on-campus lab sessions. Which statement is correct?

  • A. The course qualifies as ODL because 100 hours (83.3%) are delivered through ODL mode and the 20 hours of lab work (16.7%) are within the 20% cap
  • B. The course does not qualify because the lab sessions should not be included in an ODL course at all
  • C. The course qualifies as ODL, but the lab sessions count towards the ODL delivery percentage because they are contact hours
  • D. The course does not qualify because the independent reading hours cannot count towards ODL delivery

Summary

Physical face-to-face sessions count as contact hours and SLT but never as ODL delivery. They should be limited to a maximum of 20% of total SLT and must have a clear pedagogical, practical, professional, or regulatory justification. Not every course in an ODL programme needs to be an ODL course — courses with high physical F2F requirements may remain conventional while other courses carry the programme above the 60% threshold.
