Module 2 • Delivery Methods

Synchronous Online Learning

Real-time, instructor-led online sessions that replicate the classroom experience

You have now completed Module 1, where you learned to classify ODL programmes and courses. You know the two thresholds (>60% and ≥80%) and how to calculate them. Module 2 takes you deeper into the four delivery methods that make up those calculations. We begin with the most familiar: synchronous online learning — the method that comes closest to replicating a traditional classroom in an online environment.

What Is Synchronous Online Learning?

Synchronous online learning refers to teaching and learning activities conducted in real time through online platforms, where academic staff and students participate simultaneously and interact directly with one another regardless of their physical location.

Synchronous learning replicates many aspects of a conventional classroom environment by enabling immediate communication, discussion, feedback, collaboration, clarification of concepts, and active engagement between students and academic staff. Although lecturers and students are not physically co-located, the real-time interaction provides the immediacy and structure that characterise face-to-face teaching.

Key distinction: The word "synchronous" means at the same time. If students and the lecturer are interacting simultaneously through a digital platform, the activity is synchronous. If students access the material at different times, it is asynchronous (which we will cover on the next page).

Characteristics

For an activity to qualify as synchronous online learning, it should generally demonstrate the following characteristics:

Real-Time Interaction

Academic staff and students participate simultaneously, enabling immediate communication and feedback.

Structured Facilitation

The session is planned, guided, and facilitated by academic staff with clear learning objectives.

Active Engagement

Students are expected to participate actively through discussion, questions, activities, or collaboration.

Digital Platform

The session is conducted through an approved online platform (e.g., video conferencing, virtual classroom tools).

Examples of Synchronous Activities

The following are recognised as synchronous online learning activities because they are conducted in real time, require simultaneous participation, and provide opportunities for direct interaction:

Live Online Lectures
Virtual Tutorials
Live Webinars
Real-Time Discussions
Virtual Laboratories
Online Presentations
Live Q&A Sessions
Online Consultations

What Synchronous Online Learning Counts As

Synchronous online learning is unique among the ODL delivery methods because it counts towards three things simultaneously:

Synchronous Online Learning Counts As:

Contact Hours ODL Delivery Student Learning Time

Because synchronous sessions involve direct, real-time interaction between academic staff and students, they are recognised as the online equivalent of traditional face-to-face classroom contact. This means they qualify as contact hours for teaching and learning purposes. They also count as ODL delivery because the learning takes place through digital technologies rather than physical attendance. And they form part of the total Student Learning Time of the course.

Synchronous online learning also serves as an important mechanism for demonstrating Regular and Substantive Interaction (RSI) between academic staff and students — a key quality requirement for effective ODL delivery under MQA COPPA.

The only method that counts as contact hours and ODL delivery: Of the four delivery methods, only synchronous online learning qualifies as both. Asynchronous learning counts as ODL delivery but not contact hours. Independent learning counts as SLT but not contact hours. Physical F2F counts as contact hours but not ODL delivery.

Requirements for Counting Synchronous Hours

To ensure consistency and compliance with MQA COPPA requirements, synchronous online learning sessions should satisfy the following:

Requirements

  • Sessions must be structured and planned, with clear learning objectives aligned to Course Learning Outcomes (CLOs).
  • Sessions must be facilitated by academic staff — not simply a recording played during a scheduled time.
  • Students must be required to participate actively — passive viewing without interaction does not qualify.
  • Attendance and participation should be documented and tracked as evidence of engagement.
  • Sessions must be delivered through the University's approved digital platforms.
  • Session schedules, duration, and learning activities must be clearly indicated in Table 4 and the SIM.

Where Synchronous Learning Fits in Your Course Design

You now understand what synchronous online learning is, what it counts as, and what requirements it must meet. In practice, synchronous sessions are often the backbone of learner engagement in ODL courses — they provide the real-time human connection that keeps students motivated and gives lecturers the opportunity to clarify difficult concepts, facilitate discussion, and respond to questions immediately.

However, ODL courses should not rely on synchronous sessions alone. The case below illustrates a common pitfall when a course designer over-invests in live sessions without balancing them with other delivery methods.

Case: The Live-Only Course

Dr. Rahim designs his 3-credit ODL course (120 hours SLT) almost entirely around synchronous online sessions. He schedules three 2-hour live lectures per week across a 14-week semester (84 hours of synchronous learning). He adds 16 hours of assignment preparation as independent learning and 20 hours of online quizzes as asynchronous learning. His ODL delivery is 100%, and he is confident the course meets all requirements.

What's the concern? While Dr. Rahim's course technically meets the 80% ODL threshold (in fact, 100% of SLT is ODL), his design raises quality and practicality issues. First, 84 hours of live sessions over 14 weeks is an extremely heavy synchronous load that may not be sustainable for students in different time zones or with work commitments — flexibility is a core principle of ODL. Second, the course lacks adequate asynchronous learning materials (recorded content, structured modules, SIM) for students who miss a session or need to review. Third, only 16 hours of independent learning suggests students are not being guided to develop self-directed learning skills. A more balanced design would reduce the synchronous hours, add asynchronous materials and guided independent learning, and reserve live sessions for activities that genuinely benefit from real-time interaction.

Summary

Synchronous online learning is real-time, instructor-facilitated, and the only delivery method that counts simultaneously as contact hours, ODL delivery, and SLT. It is the backbone of real-time engagement and RSI in ODL courses. However, effective ODL design requires balancing synchronous sessions with asynchronous materials and independent learning to provide flexibility and support self-directed study.
