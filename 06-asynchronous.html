Module 2 • Delivery Methods

Asynchronous Online Learning

Structured, self-paced learning activities that give students flexibility without sacrificing guidance

On the previous page, you explored synchronous online learning — real-time, instructor-led sessions that count as contact hours, ODL delivery, and SLT. This page covers the second delivery method: asynchronous online learning. Unlike synchronous sessions, asynchronous activities do not require students and lecturers to be online at the same time. But that does not mean they are unstructured or unsupervised. As you will see, effective asynchronous learning is deliberately designed, carefully guided, and explicitly aligned with learning outcomes.

What Is Asynchronous Online Learning?

Asynchronous online learning refers to structured teaching and learning activities that are designed, facilitated, and guided by academic staff but completed by students at their own pace and time, without requiring simultaneous participation by the lecturer and students.

Asynchronous learning provides students with the flexibility to access learning materials, engage with activities, and complete assigned tasks according to their individual schedules, while still achieving the intended Course Learning Outcomes. Unlike synchronous learning, it does not require real-time interaction — but it remains a structured and planned learning activity that contributes to Student Learning Time and the overall ODL delivery requirement.

Key distinction from independent learning: Asynchronous learning is designed and facilitated by academic staff — the lecturer creates the materials, structures the activities, and guides the learning. Independent learning (next page) is self-directed by the student within a framework established by the lecturer. Both are self-paced, but the degree of staff involvement differs.

Characteristics

Structured by Academic Staff

Activities are intentionally designed with clear instructions, learning objectives, and expected outcomes.

Self-Paced

Students complete activities at their own time within defined deadlines — no simultaneous participation required.

Aligned with CLOs

Each activity contributes to the achievement of specific Course Learning Outcomes.

Digital Delivery

Activities are delivered through the LMS or other approved digital platforms, accessible from any location.

What Asynchronous Online Learning Counts As

Here is where asynchronous learning differs from synchronous. Because there is no direct real-time interaction, asynchronous activities are not classified as contact hours.

Asynchronous Online Learning Counts As:

ODL Delivery Student Learning Time Contact Hours

Even though students and lecturers do not interact simultaneously, the activities are still structured, guided, and aligned with CLOs — so they count as ODL delivery and contribute towards the 80% threshold. They also count as SLT because students are spending time engaging with learning materials and completing learning activities.

Requirements for Counting Asynchronous Hours

Requirements

  • Activities must be structured, guided, and aligned with Course Learning Outcomes.
  • Materials must be intentionally designed by academic staff — not simply uploaded lecture slides or links to external resources.
  • Instructions and learning pathways must be clear and concise so students can navigate independently.
  • Activities should include opportunities for feedback, self-assessment, or interaction (e.g., discussion forums with lecturer responses).
  • All activities must be documented in Table 4 with SLT allocations clearly indicated.

Where Asynchronous Learning Fits in Your Course Design

Asynchronous learning is an essential component of effective ODL courses. It provides the flexibility that makes distance learning possible — students in different time zones, working professionals, and those with caring responsibilities can engage with learning materials when it suits them. Well-designed asynchronous materials also serve as a lasting resource that students can revisit throughout the semester and beyond.

However, the quality of asynchronous design matters enormously. Simply uploading content without structure, guidance, or alignment to learning outcomes does not qualify. The case below illustrates a common design failure.

Case: The Content Dump

Dr. Siti uploads 40 hours' worth of PDF journal articles, 15 hours of recorded YouTube videos (not created by her), and 10 hours of PowerPoint slides from a previous semester to her LMS. She labels all 65 hours as "asynchronous online learning" in Table 4. She provides no instructions, no learning activities, no discussion forums, and no guidance on how students should engage with the materials.

What went wrong? Dr. Siti has created a content dump, not asynchronous learning. The materials lack the essential characteristics that qualify as asynchronous online learning: they are not structured around learning outcomes, there are no instructions or guided activities, no self-assessment or interaction opportunities, and no evidence of how students should engage. Simply making content available on the LMS does not constitute asynchronous learning. To fix this, Dr. Siti would need to curate the materials, create guided learning activities around them, provide clear instructions aligned with CLOs, and add interactive elements such as discussion prompts, self-check quizzes, or reflective exercises.

Summary

Asynchronous online learning is structured, staff-designed, and self-paced. It counts as ODL delivery and SLT, but not as contact hours. Effective asynchronous design requires intentional structure, clear guidance, CLO alignment, and interactive elements — not simply uploading content to the LMS. Together with synchronous sessions, asynchronous materials form the core of ODL course delivery.
