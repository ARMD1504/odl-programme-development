Why ODL Programme Development Matters

Understanding the purpose, scope, and regulatory context before you begin designing

More Than Converting Content

A common misconception is that developing an ODL programme simply means uploading lecture slides, recording video lectures, and posting them online. In reality, ODL programme development is a systematic process that requires rethinking how students learn, how teaching is delivered, and how quality is maintained — all within a digital and distance environment.

Open and Distance Learning provides students with flexible access to learning opportunities through digital technologies, self-directed learning resources, and structured academic support. It enables learners to engage regardless of geographical location and time constraints, while maintaining academic standards equivalent to conventional programme delivery.

Key principle: ODL is not a lesser version of face-to-face teaching. It is a distinct educational approach that requires its own design principles, delivery strategies, and quality assurance mechanisms.

The Regulatory Framework

ODL programmes in Malaysia operate within a well-defined regulatory environment. Before you begin designing your programme, it is important to understand the frameworks that govern ODL delivery. These frameworks ensure that ODL programmes meet national quality standards and provide students with a credible, recognised learning experience.

Key References for ODL Programme Development

  • MQA COPPA:ODL Code of Practice for Programme Accreditation: Open and Distance Learning — the primary regulatory standard for ODL programmes
  • MQF Malaysian Qualifications Framework — defines credit values, learning outcomes, and qualification levels
  • MOHE Ministry of Higher Education policies and guidelines on ODL and flexible learning
  • Institutional Sunway University's internal academic policies and quality assurance requirements

The University's ODL Programme Development and Delivery Guideline draws on all four of these sources to create a single, unified institutional reference. This guideline is the document you will work from throughout this course.

What Happens When Planning Is Skipped?

You have now seen the regulatory landscape, the purpose of the guideline, and the lifecycle stages that every ODL programme passes through. But understanding these concepts in theory is one thing — seeing what happens when they are ignored is another. The case below illustrates a real-world scenario where a programme leader treated ODL development as a simple content migration rather than a systematic design process.

Case: Dr. Farah's New ODL Programme

Dr. Farah is a programme leader who has been asked to convert her existing face-to-face Master's programme into an ODL programme. She begins by uploading her current lecture slides and recorded lectures to the LMS, believing this is sufficient for ODL delivery. When she submits her programme for review, the quality assurance team flags several issues — the programme does not meet the ODL classification requirements, Student Learning Time has not been calculated, and there is no evidence of structured learner interaction.

What went wrong? Dr. Farah treated ODL development as a content migration exercise rather than a systematic design process. She skipped the planning stage entirely — she did not classify her courses, calculate SLT, or align her delivery methods with MQA COPPA requirements. The result was a programme that looked like ODL on the surface but did not meet any of the regulatory or quality standards.

This is exactly the situation this course is designed to prevent. The pages that follow will walk you through each step that Dr. Farah missed.
