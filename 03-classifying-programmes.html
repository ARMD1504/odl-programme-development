Classifying ODL Programmes — The 60% Rule

How to determine whether your programme qualifies as an ODL programme

On the previous page, you learned the key definitions that underpin ODL delivery. You encountered two important thresholds: >60% for programmes and ≥80% for courses. This page focuses on the first of those — the programme-level threshold — and walks you through how to calculate it using real programme structures.

The Rule

A programme shall be classified as an ODL programme when more than 60% of the total graduating credits are contributed by courses where at least 80% of the course Student Learning Time (SLT) is delivered through ODL mode.

Two thresholds working together: First, each course must individually meet the 80% ODL delivery threshold to be counted as an ODL course. Then, the ODL courses must collectively contribute more than 60% of the programme's total graduating credits. Both conditions must be satisfied.

How to Calculate

The classification process follows four steps. Notice that the calculation is based on graduating credits, not the total number of courses.

  1. Identify each course's ODL status. For every course in the programme, determine whether at least 80% of its SLT is delivered through ODL approaches (synchronous online, asynchronous online, and qualifying independent learning). If yes, the course counts as an ODL course. If no, it does not.
  2. Sum the graduating credits contributed by ODL courses. Add up the credit values of all courses that qualified as ODL courses in Step 1.
  3. Calculate the percentage. Divide the ODL course credits by the total graduating credits, then multiply by 100.
  4. Apply the threshold. If the result is greater than 60%, the programme qualifies as an ODL programme.
Why graduating credits, not number of courses? Courses carry different credit values. A 6-credit course contributes more to the programme than a 2-credit course. Using graduating credits ensures that the classification reflects the actual weight of ODL delivery in the student's learning experience.

Why it qualifies: Seven of eight courses meet the 80% ODL SLT threshold. Those seven courses contribute 32 credits out of 40 graduating credits (80%), which exceeds the 60% requirement. The Industry Practicum, with only 40% ODL delivery, does not count as an ODL course — but it does not need to, because the remaining courses already carry the programme over the threshold.

Even though half the courses are ODL, the credit-weighted calculation tells a different story.

Important Considerations

  • The calculation is based on graduating credits, not the number of courses. A single high-credit course can significantly affect the outcome.
  • A programme may contain a combination of ODL courses and conventional courses. Only the ODL courses count towards the 60% threshold.
  • Courses that do not satisfy the 80% ODL delivery requirement shall not be counted as ODL courses in the calculation.
  • The ODL status of a programme shall be reviewed whenever there are significant curriculum revisions affecting course delivery modes or graduating credit structure.

Applying the Rule to Your Programme

You have now seen how the 60% rule works in theory and in practice. The next step is to apply it to your own programme. But before you do that, you need to understand the other half of the classification — how individual courses are determined to be ODL courses using the 80% rule. The two calculations are linked: you cannot classify a programme without first classifying its courses.

The case below tests whether you can spot a common error in programme-level classification.

Case: Counting Courses Instead of Credits

Dr. Azman is preparing an ODL programme proposal for a Master of Education (40 graduating credits). His programme has 10 courses, each worth different credit values. Seven of the 10 courses meet the 80% ODL SLT threshold. In his proposal, he writes: "7 out of 10 courses are ODL courses, which is 70%. This exceeds the 60% threshold, so the programme qualifies as an ODL programme."

What went wrong? Dr. Azman calculated the percentage based on the number of courses (7 out of 10 = 70%) rather than graduating credits. If the three non-ODL courses happen to carry higher credit values — say, a 6-credit practicum and two 3-credit face-to-face seminars — the ODL credits could be much lower than 70%. Suppose the ODL courses carry 3 credits each (7 × 3 = 21 credits) and the non-ODL courses carry 6, 3, and 3 credits (total 12 credits). The ODL credit share would be 21 out of 40 = 52.5%, which does not meet the 60% threshold. Dr. Azman's programme would not qualify as ODL despite his calculation suggesting otherwise.

This is one of the most common errors in ODL programme classification. Always calculate using graduating credits, never the number of courses.

Summary

Classifying an ODL programme requires calculating whether more than 60% of graduating credits come from courses that individually meet the 80% ODL delivery threshold. The calculation must be credit-weighted, not course-counted.
